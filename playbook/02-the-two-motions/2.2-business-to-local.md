# 2.2 Business → Local

> 🌐 **This is the marked exception throughout the guide.**
>
> Your customers are local businesses, but you are not tied to a place. You can sell to a plumber in Phoenix and a plumber in Pittsburgh without changing anything about how you operate.
>
> Wherever a page says **B→L differs**, that page is talking to you.

## 🌐 What Counts as Business to Local

Four things have to be true:

- 💻 **You deliver remotely, or they self-serve.** Nobody has to drive anywhere
- 🗺️ **Your customers are local businesses.** Plumbers, clinics, restaurants, contractors, salons
- ♾️ **There is no meaningful service radius.** A customer in another state costs you nothing extra to serve
- 📈 **Growth means more seats, programs or accounts**, not more vans and crews

Examples:

- 💻 **Field-service software** sold to plumbing, electrical and HVAC companies nationally
- 🎓 A **coaching or training business** for trades and contractors
- 📣 An **agency** selling marketing, web or ops work to local businesses anywhere
- 🏦 A **lender, insurer or payments provider** serving small business owners
- 📦 An **equipment or supplies company** shipping to trades across the country
- 🏢 A **franchise developer** recruiting operators in any market

## 🎁 What You Get That Local Operators Do Not

**Your market does not run out.** This is the big one. A local operator exhausts a metro in six to eight months and then has to expand geography, expand categories or stop. You open Tampa on Monday. Volume discipline becomes a choice you make rather than a limit imposed on you.

**You can actually test properly.** Running the same campaign across fifteen metros gives you the sample size a single-market operator can never reach. You can run parallel tests, get a readable answer inside a fortnight, and roll the winner out everywhere. Section 8.3 explains what that is worth.

**You pick the best verticals rather than the nearest ones.** A local cleaner takes whatever industries happen to exist in their city. You can decide that dental practices convert twice as well as restaurants and then go after every dental practice in the country.

**You can still use proximity. Just not your own.** This is the part most remote sellers throw away. Take every customer you already have and build lists in a fifteen to twenty mile radius around each one, or across the same state or region. Now the opening line writes itself: *we look after a plumbing company fifteen minutes from you*. That is the same proof a local operator uses, sourced from your customer map instead of your address. It gets stronger with every account you win, and it tells you where to sell next, because your densest regions are your easiest ones. Work outward from them rather than starting cold in a new state.

**Someone on your side can work the replies.** Most businesses in this motion have a sales function, a shared inbox and a CRM. The handoff problem that wrecks local engagements is largely not yours, and that is a structural advantage worth using rather than assuming.

## ⛓️ What Constrains You

**Your own location is not proof, so you have to borrow your customers'.** Nobody cares that your office is in Austin. What they care about is who near them already uses you, which means the proof comes from your customer map rather than from you. Where that map is thin, the copy is weaker until you fix it, and fixing it means winning the first account in a region before you can lean on the region.

**Your buyer often does not know your category exists.** A local cleaner selling to a restaurant is pitching a service the restaurant already buys from somebody. You may be pitching a category the owner has never considered, still runs on paper, or assumes is for companies bigger than theirs. That changes what the first email has to do before it can ask for anything.

**The data problem is exactly the same, and people in this motion assume it is not.** Your ICP is local even though you are not. Under 5% of local businesses sit in the standard B2B databases. Being national does not get you out of building the list properly. **Section 06 applies to you unchanged.**

**Volume makes people lazy.** A local operator with 4,000 records treats each one carefully. Give somebody 80,000 records and the temptation is to skip enrichment, skip segmentation and send one generic sequence to all of them. That produces a worse result on a bigger list, and it burns a market you cannot un-burn.

**Scale brings its own admin.** Duplicate accounts across metros, the same multi-location group appearing eleven times, time zones affecting send windows, and suppression that has to work nationally rather than across one city.

## 🔧 What This Changes in Practice

| Where | What it means for you |
| --- | --- |
| **Sourcing** (6.3) | Go wide across metros, and build rings around the customers you already have. Category depth is your lever, not ZIP-level decomposition |
| **Sizing** (3.3) | Your ceiling is fulfilment capacity, not market size. The sizing check becomes a sanity check |
| **Offer** (07) | Category education first, then ROI. Peer proof instead of proximity |
| **Copy** (9.1) | Lower awareness level. Part of email one has to establish that a solution exists at all |
| **Personalization** (9.4) | Proximity still works, anchored on your customers rather than your office. A 15 to 20 mile radius around an existing account gives you the same opening line a local operator uses |
| **Testing** (8.3) | You can run parallel tests. Borrow volume across metros and get a real answer fast |
| **Channels** (12) | Direct mail still works but the cost scales with reach. LinkedIn becomes viable on tier two |
| **Suppression** (6.7) | Duplicate accounts and multi-location groups, not territory conflicts |

## ⚠️ Where Business to Local Operators Get It Wrong

- 🕳️ **Assuming the local data problem is not theirs.** It is. Your ICP is the same set of businesses missing from every standard database, and pulling them from one is how you end up emailing the same 200 companies your competitors are emailing
- 🪣 **Building one giant national list and sending one sequence.** Bigger list, worse result. Segment by metro, by tier and by contact bucket before you write anything
- 🔩 **Leading with the mechanism.** Feature-first copy assumes the reader already wants the category. Yours often does not know it exists. Outcome first, always. Section 9.1
- 🌍 **Not mapping your customers.** "3,000 businesses use us" is far weaker than "we look after a plumbing company fifteen minutes from you." If you are not sourcing in rings around your existing accounts, you are throwing away the only proximity proof available to you
- 🏬 **Getting the tier target wrong.** With multi-location groups, locations are easy to reach and often cannot buy, while head office can buy and is harder to reach. Decide before you build. Section 0.3
- 🕐 **Ignoring time zones.** A send window tuned to your morning lands at 5am for a third of your list

## 🏠 If This Does Not Sound Like You

If a won deal means somebody from your team has to drive there, you are running the other motion. Read **2.1 Local → Local** instead.

The data layer is identical either way. Almost everything downstream is not.
